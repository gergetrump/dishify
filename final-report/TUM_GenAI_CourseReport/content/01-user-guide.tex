    2.2~million recipes for the dishes that best fit both your craving and your
    shelf.
\end{minipage}

\vspace{6pt}
\noindent
Every suggestion arrives with a plain-language answer to two questions:
\emph{why does this fit me}, and \emph{what am I missing}. Recipes that clash
with a stored allergy or diet are removed before you ever see them. Dishify is
built for everyday home cooks who own food but not a plan, and who lose more
time to deciding than to cooking.

\section{Getting Started}

Dishify runs as a web application and as a native iOS app; both talk to the
same backend, so a pantry and preferences set on one are the same account on
the other.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{Open the app.} Go to \url{http://167.233.165.44} in any
          modern browser (Figure~\ref{fig:ug-welcome}). No installation is
          needed. iOS users open the Dishify app instead.
    \item \textbf{Create an account.} Select \emph{Sign up} and choose a
          username, e-mail, and password. Accounts are managed by Keycloak;
          Dishify never stores your password itself.
    \item \textbf{Set what you must avoid} (recommended, and the one step worth
          doing carefully) under \emph{Preferences}; see
          Section~\ref{sec:ug-restrictions}.
    \item \textbf{Add what you have} under \emph{Cook}, then press
          \emph{Find my recipes}.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
A results page normally appears in a few seconds. For evaluation purposes, a
reviewer account is available on the deployed instance: username
\texttt{testuser}, password \texttt{test-secret}.

\section{Features}

\subsection{Pantry-based Recipe Recommendation}

The \emph{Cook} screen (Figure~\ref{fig:ug-pantry}) is split into two
questions: \emph{what is in your kitchen} and \emph{what are you craving}.

Add ingredients one at a time with the \emph{Add ingredient} button. Quantity
and unit are optional, but supplying them is worthwhile: a recipe needing
$500$\,g of pasta when you have $200$\,g counts as a partial rather than a full
match, and ranks accordingly. Items collect on \emph{Your pantry shelf}, where
they can be edited or deleted, and they persist between visits so you do not
retype a standing pantry.

The craving field is free text and entirely optional. Examples such as ``quick
weeknight pasta dinner'' or ``something warm and cheap'' are enough, and the
\emph{Results} spinner sets how many suggestions to return.

\subsection{Voice Input and Photo Scanning}

Typing five ingredients is tedious, so Dishify accepts two faster inputs from
the same screen (Figure~\ref{fig:ug-capture}).

\paragraph{Say what you have.} Press the button and speak naturally:
\emph{``I have two tomatoes, some pasta and garlic, and I want something
quick.''} Dishify transcribes the clip, splits it into a structured ingredient
list \emph{and} a craving, and fills in both fields at once.

\paragraph{Scan ingredients.} Point your camera at an open fridge or a
worktop, or upload a photo. Dishify detects the edible items in the image and
proposes them as pantry entries.

\noindent
Both paths are deliberately \emph{review-then-confirm}: detected items appear
as a proposed list that you correct before anything is searched, so a misheard
word or a misread jar never silently changes your results.

\subsection{Dietary, Allergy, and Restriction Filtering}
\label{sec:ug-restrictions}

\emph{Preferences} (Figure~\ref{fig:ug-preferences}) is where Dishify learns
what it must never suggest. It offers 72 tags in five groups: allergies,
diets, religious and ethical rules, medical sensitivities, and broad ingredient
exclusions. Together they cover everything from nut allergy and coeliac disease to
halal, kosher, and low-FODMAP.

These are \textbf{hard filters}, not hints. A recipe carrying a conflicting
ingredient is removed during the search itself, so it cannot appear in your
results, cannot be re-ranked back in, and is never passed to the language model
for explanation. Set them once; they apply to every later search on every
device, and you never need to restate them in a query.

\subsection{Reading Your Results}

Results are ranked cards (Figure~\ref{fig:ug-results}). Each one shows:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
    \item a \textbf{match strength} score blending how well the recipe answers
          your craving with how much of it you can already cook;
    \item \textbf{Why it fits} and \textbf{Watch for}, a short, generated
          justification grounded in the recipe's own ingredients;
    \item green \textbf{HAVE} and amber \textbf{NEED} chips, plus an
          ``\emph{n} matched $\cdot$ \emph{m} missing'' count, so the shopping
          gap is visible at a glance.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
A collapsible \emph{Pipeline details} panel at the foot of the page reports
what each stage of the system did and how long it took. This is useful when
you want to know whether an explanation came from the language model or from
the built-in fallback.

\subsection{Cooking a Recipe}

Opening a card gives the full recipe (Figure~\ref{fig:ug-detail}): the
ingredient match, the reasoning, and the directions.

Source recipes are often terse. The original of the dish shown reads, in full,
``\emph{In a bowl, mix pasta (cooked) of your choice with mixture and serve
immediately.}'' Dishify rewrites such directions into numbered,
beginner-ready steps with per-step timings, an estimated total, and occasional
technique tips, without inventing ingredients the recipe does not imply.

\section{Tips and Troubleshooting}

\begin{description}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt, leftmargin=*, style=nextline]
    \item[Set your restrictions before your first search.] They are the one
          setting that changes what Dishify is allowed to show you.
    \item[List staples too.] Adding salt, oil, and pepper to the shelf
          noticeably shortens the ``NEED'' list on most cards.
    \item[Keep the craving short.] Two or three words (``quick'', ``warm'',
          ``cheap'') steer the search well; long sentences dilute it.
    \item[The first search of a session can be slower.] The search service
          loads its embedding model on first use; later searches are faster.
    \item[Explanations look plain?] If the language model is unreachable, or
          returns something that cannot be verified against the recipe, Dishify
          falls back to a factual matched/missing summary rather than showing
          an unchecked explanation. Results stay usable.
\end{description}
